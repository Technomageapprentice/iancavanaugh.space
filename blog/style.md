# Blog Style Guide

**Voice, structure, and principles for iancavanaugh.space/blog**

---

## Inspirations

| Blog | What to steal |
|---|---|
| [Experimental History](https://www.experimental-history.com) — Adam Mastroianni | Personal flair, self-deprecating humor, tooltip footnotes, direct address |
| [SatPost](https://www.satpost.com) — Trung Phan | Research depth, relatable framing of complex ideas |
| [Gwern](https://gwern.net) | Information density, concept linking, trust the reader to go deeper |

---

## Voice

First person throughout. Dry. No hedging — make the claim, let it stand. Self-deprecating with a bit of ego: acknowledge blind spots openly, but don't perform humility. The blog is the personal register of the site. The portfolio is the resume. The blog is the person.

**What this sounds like:**
- "I got this wrong for two years before I understood why."
- "The estimators stopped doing the math and started checking it."
- A dry footnote the main text doesn't need but is better for having.

**What this doesn't sound like:**
- "In this post, I'll explore..."
- "It's important to note that..."
- Anything that would appear on a LinkedIn thought leadership carousel.

---

## Structure

Every post follows a loose shape. Structure serves the argument — don't impose it when the content doesn't need it.

1. **Epigraph** — required (see below)
2. **Hook** — a scene, a question, or a contradiction. No thesis yet.
3. **Thesis** — stated plainly, once the reader is oriented.
4. **Development** — argument, evidence, example. Footnotes live here.
5. **Concrete example** — own work where applicable. Don't assert the principle; show where it ran.
6. **Close** — actionable or provocative. Short. No summary paragraph.
7. **Sign-off**

**Target length:** 800–1,500 words. Long enough to develop an idea, short enough to respect the reader's time.

---

## Epigraphs

Required. One per post, before the body.

**The job:** Intellectual signaling that doesn't explain itself. The connection to the thesis becomes clear during or after reading — not before. The epigraph is the oblique angle.

**The model:** Watership Down's chapter epigraphs — quotes from a parallel world that illuminate this one without annotating it. The invisible chapter architecture of Ulysses — structure doing work the reader never sees. The epigraph rewards the reader who recognizes the source. It doesn't punish the one who doesn't.

**The test:** After reading the post, does the epigraph feel inevitable? If it still feels random, swap it. When it works, it's the best sentence on the page.

**Publishing gate:** No great epigraph, no publish. If the post is done and the epigraph isn't landing, the post isn't done. The difficulty is the point — an epigraph that won't come is a signal the thesis isn't sharp enough yet.

**Draw from:**
- Engineering and systems literature (Deming, Reason, Shingo, Norman)
- Philosophy, political theory, history of science
- Fiction and poetry — the unexpected source that turns out to be exactly right
- Obscure technical history; primary sources over summaries

**Avoid:**
- Business books
- Anything that has appeared on a motivational poster
- Quotes that summarize the thesis directly — that's a caption, not an epigraph

## Footnotes

Tooltip style. Three jobs, sometimes in the same post:

1. **Humor** — the dry aside that would interrupt the main text's momentum. The footnote is where the smirk lives. Don't kill the joke by putting it in the body.
2. **Depth** — for the reader who wants to go further. Links, context, the Gwern-style rabbit hole. Don't over-explain in the body; offer the thread in the footnote.
3. **Citation** — source plus brief context. Not just a URL. One sentence on why it matters.

Footnotes should feel like marginalia from someone paying close attention, not endnotes from someone covering their bases.

---

## Concept Linking

Link to related posts and external sources where an idea is developed further. Don't explain what's at the other end of the link — trust the reader to decide whether to follow it. Over-explanation is condescension dressed as helpfulness.

As the archive grows, link backward to earlier posts where a concept first appeared. Build the web deliberately. The goal is a body of work that rewards re-reading, not a feed.

---

## Sign-Off

*A flourish, a fumble, a smirk, and a shrug.*

Keep it. Four words doing exactly what the voice does: ambition (flourish), honesty about the result (fumble), awareness of the gap (smirk), refusal to be precious about it (shrug). It's a stance, not a comment on the specific post. It will get more meaningful the longer the blog runs.

---

## What to Avoid

- Lists as a substitute for argument
- Passive voice as a hedge
- Epigraphs that explain themselves
- Burying the thesis in qualifications
- Ending on a summary — the last sentence should be the sharpest sentence in the post
- Anything that sounds like it was written to impress someone on LinkedIn
